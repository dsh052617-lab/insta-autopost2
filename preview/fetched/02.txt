Minutes released: 'Almost all' participants backed additional tightening
Some officials said hike of more than 25bp possible if inflation worsens
The Federal Reserve held its benchmark interest rate steady at its October FOMC meeting, but minutes from the gathering show broad agreement among policymakers that further rate increases will be needed.
Some officials said they were open to a 50-basis-point "big step" increase — twice the standard increment — if inflation data deteriorate more than expected.
According to minutes from the Oct. 27-28 FOMC meeting, released Wednesday (local time), almost all participants judged that additional rate hikes would be necessary in the coming months to bring inflation back to the 2 percent target in a timely manner.
At that meeting, the Fed held its benchmark interest rate at 3.75 to 4.00 percent. Two members — Fed Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Federal Reserve Bank President Beth Hammack — dissented, calling for a 25-basis-point increase.
A couple of participants said at the meeting that, if incoming inflation data came in worse than expected, they could consider raising rates by more than 25 basis points at the next meeting.
A few participants also said inflation could prove more persistent than anticipated, which might require a shift to a more restrictive policy stance.
Even some of those who supported the hold described it as "a close call."
Officials who had favored a rate increase said they could have supported holding rates steady, provided that market participants clearly understood that further tightening remained likely.
Many participants said it was critically important to communicate clearly to markets that the decision to pause did not signal any weakening of the Fed's commitment to returning inflation to 2 percent in a timely manner.
Participants broadly agreed that upward price pressures remain strong.
They said that while tariff-related effects have eased somewhat, inflationary pressures across the broader economy remain widespread.
High energy prices were also seen as continuing to add to upward pressure on inflation.
On AI, participants said demand for AI infrastructure is straining supply chains and pushing up consumer goods prices.
Several participants expressed concern that these price pressures are not confined to the data center construction sector but are gradually spreading across the broader economy.
With a December rate hike widely seen in markets as a foregone conclusion, attention has shifted to the size and pace of further tightening.
Markets were rattled Nov. 10 when Fed Governor Christopher Waller became the first official since the September FOMC to raise the possibility of a big-step hike. Waller reiterated the point that day, saying the Fed may need "to shock people."
New York Federal Reserve Bank President John Williams, by contrast, drew a line against any "extreme measures," even as he stressed the importance of price stability.
